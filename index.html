What needs to change
when you talk again?

You're trying to work things out with your partner, but the same disagreement about money or responsibilities keeps coming back.

Or you're parenting after divorce, and a decision about your kid turns into another argument about the past.

You want your point understood. Start by separating the issue you need to resolve from the accusation you want to answer.

Practice the part you can't script.

You can prepare your words. The harder part comes when the reply isn't what you wanted to hear. ARC gives you a sequence to practice at that point: notice your reaction, pause, and choose what to do.

  1. Aware

    Notice when you start building a defense, interrupting, or going quiet to end the conversation.

  2. Release

    Take a breath before answering. If you need a break, say when you can return to the conversation.

  3. Choose & Commit

    Say what you need and hear the response. Be clear about what you can agree to, then do what you said you would.

What could you say differently?

With your partner

“I'm stretched thin in the evenings. Can we work out dinner and bedtime together? I'll take dinner tonight.”

With your ex

“I want us to decide on activities before either of us signs him up. Can we look at the cost and the days first?”

Both replies put a specific need on the table. What happens next matters too: hearing the answer, holding a boundary where needed, and keeping any agreement you make.

I thought I was the good guy.
Then I saw the video.

My wife at the time filmed me during an argument. I thought I was making my point. On screen, I saw myself using anger to control her. My first instinct was to delete the video.

I could recognize the pattern and still repeat it. Even after I learned to stop raising my voice, I could bury resentment or pull away when I didn't like what I was hearing. Changing meant working on those reactions, too.

What changed in everyday life

When Winnie's garage door broke, we weren't living together yet. My first thought was that it wasn't my job. I caught that thought, found someone to repair the door, negotiated the price, and saw the job through.

It was a small job. But I had followed through instead of finding a reason to leave it to someone else. That gave me something concrete to repeat.

ARC grew through my relationship with Winnie and the work she was already doing. Noticing a reaction, pausing, and choosing what to do next became our shared practice. It's the work we bring to Heart Center LA.

What happens in the four sessions?

  1. Find where you get stuck

    Bring a recent disagreement. Identify what needed resolving, how you responded, and what remained unfinished. Choose one thing to work on.

  2. Prepare for the real reply

    Practice stating your need or boundary, then responding to disagreement. Use a brief breathing or movement practice to pause before answering.

  3. Review the conversation and the follow-through

    Look at what you said, what you heard, and what you did afterward. Adjust your approach. If the conversation hasn't happened, rehearse the situation with Jay.

  4. Keep practicing outside the sessions

    Apply what you've learned to another situation. Leave with a personal ARC practice and a written plan for your next conversation and commitments.

Coaching focuses on your responses and actions. It is not couples therapy, mediation, or legal advice, and cannot guarantee another person's cooperation or the outcome of a relationship.
